Warranty vs Guarantee Under Thai Law — Complete Guide

Thundthornthep Yaem-Uthai, Ph.D. | LAS Legal | Published 3 April 2026 | Last updated 28 September 2026 | ภาษาไทย

"Warranty" and "Guarantee" sound similar — but under Thai law they are entirely different legal instruments. Many business operators use these terms interchangeably without realising that the legal consequences are radically different. This article covers everything from the statutory framework — TCCC Sections 472–474 (Warranty) and Sections 680–701 (Guarantee) — to Bank Guarantees, Parent Company Guarantees, common drafting mistakes, landmark Supreme Court decisions, and a 6-question FAQ.

Warranty — Quality Assurance Under TCCC Sections 472–474

In the context of a sale of goods, a Warranty — or more precisely, the warranty against defects — is the seller's obligation to deliver goods of the agreed quality, free of defects. TCCC Book 3, Title 1 (Sale of Property), Chapter 2, Part 2, provides the statutory framework for the seller's warranty obligations.

Section 472 establishes the crucial principle that the seller's warranty liability is independent of the seller's knowledge. Even if the seller was unaware of the defect, liability still attaches — provided the defect existed at the time of delivery. This differs fundamentally from fraud or criminal liability, which requires proof of intent.

Types of "Defect" Recognised Under Thai Law

  • Defects reducing the value of the goods: The goods are in a worse condition than they should be — e.g., a vehicle with undisclosed mechanical faults.
  • Defects reducing fitness for ordinary use: The goods cannot be used for their intended purpose — e.g., machinery that cannot produce at the advertised specification.
  • Defects inconsistent with the contract specification: The goods do not meet the specific requirements agreed in the contract.

🔴 Risk: High — Warranty Exclusions That Are Void

TCCC Section 485 provides that if the seller knew of the defect but concealed it, the exclusion cannot protect the seller from liability for the concealed fact. It also cannot protect the seller from consequences of the seller's own acts. Option A: Buyers should require the seller to give an express representation that there are no hidden defects. | Option B: In M&A transactions, include an Indemnification clause specifically for Latent Defects that are not disclosed.

Warranty Limitations Requiring Legal Review

  • "As Is Where Is": Selling in the current condition, as inspected — subject to Sections 473 and 483–485 and applicable unfair-contract-terms protections; inspection alone does not validate every exclusion.
  • Contractual claim notification: An agreed notification deadline must be distinguished from statutory limitation. Section 193/11 prohibits agreements to waive, extend or shorten a statutory limitation period; the validity of any contractual notice condition requires separate review.
  • Cap on damages: Limiting damages to the purchase price or a percentage thereof.
  • Exclusion of indirect damages: Any exclusion of consequential loss or lost profits requires review under the applicable mandatory law; business-to-business status does not make every exclusion enforceable.

Guarantee — Debt Guarantee Under TCCC Sections 680-701

TCCC Section 680 defines a guarantee as follows: "A contract of suretyship is a contract whereby a person, called the surety, binds himself to a creditor to discharge an obligation in case the debtor fails to discharge it."

Elements of a Guarantee Agreement

  • Three parties: Creditor — Principal Debtor — Guarantor (Surety)
  • Must be in writing: TCCC Section 680, paragraph 2 requires written evidence signed by the guarantor for court enforcement. Qualifying electronic records and electronic signatures may satisfy these requirements under electronic-transactions law.
  • The guaranteed debt must exist or be determinable: Under Section 681, the guaranteed debt or contract must be expressly identified. A guarantee of future or conditional debt must state its purpose, nature, maximum guaranteed amount and period for incurring the debt; a continuing guarantee under Section 699 may omit that period.
  • Accessory nature: The guarantee is an accessory contract to the principal debt. Section 698 releases the guarantor when the principal debt is extinguished.

Termination of the Guarantee Agreement

Circumstances in Which the Guarantor Is Released

  • Principal debt extinguished: When the debtor fully repays the debt, the guarantee terminates automatically.
  • Creditor grants extension of time without guarantor's consent: Section 700 concerns an extension of debt payable at a fixed time without guarantor consent. Review its limits on advance consent, its financial-institution/professional-guarantor exception and the debt-reduction exception in Section 691.
  • Creditor allows the security to deteriorate: Under Section 697, if the creditor's conduct prevents the guarantor from exercising subrogation rights, the guarantor is released to the extent of the resulting loss.
  • Guarantee has a fixed term: Check whether the end date limits new debts or also affects existing liabilities; expiry does not necessarily extinguish liability for debts already covered.
  • Guarantor gives notice of termination: In a continuing guarantee with no fixed term, the guarantor may terminate by giving reasonable notice.

Bank Guarantee vs Personal Guarantee

In significant commercial transactions, creditors typically require a guarantee from a reliable source. There are two main types:

Bank Guarantee — Letter of Guarantee from a Bank

A Bank Guarantee is a letter issued by a bank to its customer, assuring a third party (the beneficiary) that the bank will pay the specified amount if the customer (the principal) fails to fulfil its obligations.

Some Bank Guarantees are ambiguous about the conditions for demand — e.g., what documents must be submitted, whether proof of default is required, or whether it is an Unconditional (First Demand) Guarantee. Parent Company Guarantee

In corporate transactions — particularly in multinational groups or multi-entity SME structures — creditors often require a Parent Company Guarantee (PCG) from the holding company. The PCG assures the creditor that if the subsidiary that is the contracting party cannot pay, the parent company will step in.

Key Features of a Parent Company Guarantee

  • Corporate Guarantee: A legal entity (the parent) is the guarantor — a board resolution approving the guarantee is required before signing.
  • On Demand vs Conditional: Specify whether the guarantee is payable immediately on demand or only after the subsidiary's default is established.
  • Continuing Guarantee: The PCG typically covers all debts arising from the business relationship, not just a single transaction.
  • Change of control provisions: Specify whether a change in ownership, management, or corporate restructuring of the subsidiary will affect the PCG.
Key Checks for a Parent Company Guarantee:
  • Does the parent's articles of association permit it to provide guarantees?
  • Is there a board resolution (or shareholder resolution, if required) approving the guarantee?
  • If the parent is a foreign company, is the governing law and jurisdiction specified?
  • Is the Maximum Liability Amount (cap) specified?

Enforcement of a Guarantee — Step-by-Step

When a debtor defaults, the creditor must follow the correct procedure to enforce against the guarantor:

  1. Confirm that the debtor has actually defaulted: Identify the due date and confirm non-payment.
  2. Send written notice to the guarantor: The creditor must send written notice within 60 days from the debtor's default and may not demand payment before notice reaches the guarantor. Late notice releases the guarantor from interest, damages and accessories arising after the 60-day deadline, rather than from all principal liability. Section 685/1 makes agreements differing from Section 686 void.
  3. Demand payment from the guarantor: If the guarantor has waived the right of discussion, the creditor may demand directly from the guarantor without first pursuing the debtor.
  4. If payment is not made — commence proceedings: File a claim in the court of competent jurisdiction, supported by the guarantee agreement, evidence of default, and proof of the Section 686 notice.

Warranty in M&A Transactions — Representations and Warranties

In Share Purchase Agreements (SPAs) and Asset Purchase Agreements (APAs), Representations and Warranties (R&W) are at the heart of the transaction. They are the seller's contractual assurance of the facts about the business being sold — the basis on which the buyer makes its acquisition decision.

Categories of Representations and Warranties

  • Corporate Warranties: Confirm due incorporation, authority, articles of association, board resolutions
  • Financial Warranties: Confirm accuracy of financial statements; no hidden liabilities
  • Title Warranties: Confirm ownership of assets; no encumbrances
  • Operational Warranties: Confirm key contracts, customer/supplier relationships, no pending disputes
  • IP Warranties: Confirm ownership of all intellectual property
  • Employment Warranties: Confirm employment contracts; no pending labour claims
  • Tax Warranties: Confirm tax compliance; no unpaid taxes

Sample Warranty Clause — M&A Agreement Template

(a) The Company has been duly incorporated and remains validly subsisting under applicable law;

Continuing Guarantee vs Specific Transaction Guarantee

Continuing Guarantee

  • Covers all debts arising from the ongoing relationship between the creditor and debtor — not limited to a single transaction.
  • Common in bank facilities and revolving credit lines.
  • Risk: The guarantor may not know the total accumulated debt at any given time.
  • Always specify a Maximum Liability Cap in the agreement.

Specific Transaction Guarantee

  • Guarantees a specific identified debt — e.g., Loan Tranche [x] in the amount of [x] Baht.
  • When that specific debt is repaid, the guarantee terminates automatically.
  • Lower risk for the guarantor — the scope is clearly defined from the outset.
🟡 Risk: Medium — Continuing Guarantee Without a Cap Amount

For future or conditional debts, Section 681 requires a maximum guaranteed amount and a clearly identified debt or contract. A continuing guarantee does not remove that requirement. An omitted cap therefore raises a validity and scope issue, rather than automatically creating unlimited liability. Option A: Always specify a Maximum Liability Amount — e.g., "The Guarantor's liability shall not exceed [x] Baht in aggregate." | Option B: Include a Review Period requiring the creditor to disclose the current outstanding balance to the guarantor every six months.

Common Mistakes in Warranty and Guarantee Practice

Warranty Mistakes

  • Buyer fails to inspect goods before accepting delivery: Makes it harder to prove that the defect existed before delivery — always inspect and record the condition of goods at the time of handover.
  • Notifying the defect too late: The one-year limitation period under Section 474 runs from the date of discovery — act immediately.
  • No Warranty clause in the contract: The law imposes warranty obligations automatically, but an explicit contractual warranty clause is clearer, especially in M&A transactions.
  • Confusing Warranty with Indemnity: Warranty is a contractual assurance; remedies depend on the applicable law and breach. Indemnity allocates specified losses, but does not automatically dispense with proof. Contractual notification conditions must be distinguished from statutory limitation periods.

Guarantee Mistakes

  • Guarantee without qualifying signed written evidence: Court enforcement requires signed written evidence under Section 680, paragraph 2; qualifying electronic evidence is not automatically excluded.
  • Failure to specify the scope of the guaranteed debt: Creates disputes about which obligations are covered. Always define the guaranteed debt precisely.
  • Granting extension of time to the debtor without notifying the guarantor: For debt payable at a fixed time, Section 700 releases the guarantor upon an extension without consent. Advance consent is generally ineffective, with the statutory exception for financial institutions or persons regularly guaranteeing for remuneration. Section 691 also contains an exception for specified post-default debt-reduction agreements.
  • Forgetting the Section 686 notice obligation: Late notice releases interest, damages and accessories arising after the 60-day deadline under Section 686; the protection cannot be waived.
  • Failure to verify the director's authority to sign the guarantee: A director who signs a corporate guarantee without a valid board resolution approving it may not bind the company — and may face personal liability. Always obtain and verify the board resolution.

Illustrative Scenarios — Thai SMEs and Warranty / Guarantee

Case 1: Defective Machinery — Potential Warranty Claim (Company A)

Company A purchased food processing machinery worth THB 5 million. The contract included a 1-year Warranty. Eight months after delivery, the machinery malfunctioned and caused product spoilage. The seller argued that misuse by Company A's operators caused the damage.

🟡 Dispute

Company A sued under Warranty (TCCC Sections 472–474), claiming the defect existed prior to delivery.

Outcome: In this illustrative scenario, Company A would need technical evidence that the defect was inherent rather than caused by misuse. Any remedy and compensation depend on the proven breach, loss, applicable law and limitation period; no reported judgment is asserted here.

Lesson: Always conduct a thorough Acceptance Test before taking delivery, document the condition in writing, and keep records throughout the Warranty period.

Case 2: Office Lease — Director Guarantee and the Section 686 Notice (Company B)

Company B leased commercial office space. The company's director signed a separate personal guarantee. Company B defaulted on rent. The landlord immediately sued the director as guarantor — without giving the written default notice required under TCCC Section 686.

🔴 Critical Issue

The director challenged the claim on the ground that the Section 686 notice was not given within 60 days of default.

Outcome: Before notice reaches the guarantor, the landlord cannot demand payment from the guarantor. If notice is not sent within 60 days after default, the guarantor is released from interest, damages and accessories arising after that deadline; this does not itself extinguish the principal debt.

Option A (Creditor): Always calendar the Section 686 notice obligation immediately upon any default. | Option B (Guarantor): Before accepting liability, verify whether all Section 686 procedural requirements were fulfilled by the creditor.

Case 3: M&A — Parent Company Guarantee Without a Cap (Company C)

In an M&A transaction, the parent company (Company C Parent) issued a Parent Company Guarantee covering "all obligations of the subsidiary." After closing, the subsidiary became insolvent. The buyer enforced the guarantee, claiming not only the purchase price shortfall but also all post-closing liabilities — including interest arrears, penalties, and legal costs. No Maximum Liability Amount had been specified in the guarantee.

🟡 Outcome

The legal issue is whether the future-debt guarantee satisfies Section 681, including the maximum-amount requirement. Liability cannot be assumed to be unlimited solely because the document omits a cap; accessories and litigation costs must also be assessed under Sections 683–684.

Lesson: Always specify a Maximum Liability Amount in any Parent Company Guarantee. Consider adding a Sunset Clause limiting the guarantee to a fixed period after closing.

Checklist — 10 Points Before Signing a Warranty or Guarantee Clause

  1. Define the scope of Warranty clearly: Specify exactly what is warranted — quality, performance, fitness for purpose, title. Avoid vague language.
  2. Set the Warranty period and starting date: State whether the warranty period runs from delivery date, installation date, or Acceptance Test date.
  3. Specify the Warranty claim procedure: How many days to notify? In what form? What evidence must accompany the claim?
  4. For Guarantee: written document, signed by guarantor — mandatory: A purely oral promise is insufficient under Section 680, paragraph 2. Qualifying electronic records with electronic signatures may satisfy the written-evidence requirement.
  5. Define the scope of the guaranteed debt precisely: Which obligations are covered? Which are excluded? State clearly.
  6. Always specify a Maximum Liability Amount (Cap): Section 681 requires a maximum amount for future or conditional debt. Sections 683–684 govern accessories and litigation costs; do not assume that an omitted cap makes liability unlimited.
  7. Set a clear Guarantee end date or Sunset Clause: Continuing guarantees without a time limit create open-ended liability.
  8. Verify signing authority: For corporate guarantors — obtain and verify the board resolution; check the articles of association for restrictions.
  9. Implement Section 686 compliance system: Record the actual default date, send written notice within 60 days after default, and retain evidence that notice reached the guarantor before demanding payment.
  10. Have a lawyer review before signing: Especially for high-value guarantees, Parent Company Guarantees in M&A transactions, and any guarantee without a cap.
🟢 Best Practice — Use Warranty and Guarantee Together

In significant contracts, Warranty and Guarantee may be used in combination. For example, in an M&A agreement: R&W Clause (seller warrants the quality of the business) together with an Escrow Account or Parent Company Guarantee (to ensure the seller can actually pay damages if a warranty is breached). This dual-layer approach gives the buyer both quality assurance and enforcement certainty.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1: What is the practical difference between Warranty and Guarantee?
Warranty is a direct quality assurance by the seller to the buyer — a two-party obligation governed by TCCC Sections 472–474. No written document is required; liability arises by operation of law. Guarantee is a three-party arrangement in which a third party (the guarantor) promises to pay the debt of the principal debtor if the debtor defaults — governed by TCCC Sections 680–701. Signed written evidence is required for court enforcement; qualifying electronic records and signatures may satisfy this requirement.
Q2: How is a Bank Guarantee different from an ordinary guarantee?
A Bank Guarantee is issued by a bank (financial institution) as guarantor. Whether the instrument is an independent first-demand guarantee or a conditional accessory guarantee depends on its wording and legal character. Waiver of a right does not by itself remove other mandatory protections. Section 690 concerns enforcement against security held by the creditor; Section 689 concerns enforcement against the debtor's assets under specified conditions.
Q3: How can a guarantor be released from liability under Thai law?
A guarantor is released in several circumstances: (1) the principal debt is extinguished — the debtor pays in full; (2) an extension of fixed-time debt without consent, subject to the exceptions in TCCC Sections 700 and 691; (3) the creditor's conduct prevents the guarantor from exercising subrogation rights (Sec. 697); (4) the creditor fails to give timely notice under Section 686 (releases interest, damages and accessories arising after the 60-day deadline); or (5) the agreed scope ends, subject to liabilities for debts already covered.
Q4: What Warranty provisions should an M&A agreement contain?
In an M&A agreement, the seller should provide Representations and Warranties covering: due incorporation and corporate authority; accuracy of financial statements; no pending or threatened litigation; title to all assets (no encumbrances); IP ownership; compliance with all laws; employment contracts (no outstanding claims); and tax compliance. The agreement should also include an Indemnification clause specifying the remedy for warranty breach, the applicable cap on liability, and the notification deadline for claims.
Q5: What is required for a director to sign a guarantee on behalf of a company?
Three things must be verified: (1) a valid board resolution (or shareholder resolution if required) expressly approving the guarantee; (2) the company's articles of association do not prohibit the company from providing guarantees; and (3) the directors signing have authority to sign together under the articles — e.g., if the articles require two directors to sign jointly, both must sign. If any of these is missing, the guarantee may not bind the company, and the signing directors may face personal liability.
Q6 (Bonus): If a guarantee agreement does not specify a maximum amount, how much is the guarantor liable for?
Section 682 concerns sub-suretyship and joint liability of co-sureties. Section 683 extends an unlimited guarantee to interest, damages and accessories of the guaranteed debt. Section 684 addresses litigation costs, excluding those costs where the creditor sued without first demanding payment from the guarantor. Section 681 requires a maximum guaranteed amount for future or conditional debt; an omitted cap does not automatically create unlimited liability.
Q7: If a Continuing Guarantee is terminated by the guarantor, what happens to outstanding debts?
A guarantor may terminate a Continuing Guarantee by giving written notice to the creditor under TCCC Section 699. However, termination only affects future debts — the guarantor remains liable for debts that were already incurred before the date of termination. To limit exposure, guarantors should terminate as soon as they decide they no longer wish to cover future obligations, and should document the exact date and method of notification.
Q8: In an M&A transaction, if a Warranty breach is discovered after Closing, what enforcement mechanisms are available to the buyer?
Three principal mechanisms are available: (1) Indemnification Claim — the buyer sends a Written Warranty Notice within the agreed Claim Period, setting out the breach and the claimed damages; (2) Escrow Draw-Down — if a portion of the purchase price was held in escrow, the buyer may draw against the escrow immediately upon proving a breach; (3) Price Adjustment — a downward adjustment of the purchase price proportional to the proven breach. The choice of mechanism and the applicable limitation period will be governed by the specific terms of the Share Purchase Agreement or Asset Purchase Agreement. Post-closing Warranty claims are almost always time-limited — act within the contractual claim window, which is typically shorter than the general statute of limitations.

Summary

Warranty and Guarantee are fundamentally different legal instruments. Warranty is a direct quality assurance by the seller under TCCC Sections 472–474. Guarantee is a three-party accessory agreement under TCCC Sections 680–701 — and requires signed written evidence for court enforcement. Understanding the distinction, choosing the right instrument for the situation, and following the correct procedure — especially the 60-day Section 686 notice rule — are essential to enforcing these instruments effectively.
