Nominee Shareholding in Thailand:
Legal Risks Every Business Owner Must Know ✓

✓ Legal content last reviewed 14 October 2026 · website content updated 14 October 2026

Legal analysis of nominee shareholding problems in Thai limited companies under the Civil and Commercial Code, with the rules stated from the statute and preventive recommendations

Thundthornthep Yamoutai, Ph.D. Legal Advance Solution Co., Ltd. April 2026
Part 1: Nominee Shareholding Part 2: Nominee Directors

1. Introduction

In the Thai business landscape, it is common practice for individuals to have others hold shares on their behalf (Nominee Shareholding). This arrangement may arise from various business or personal reasons. Nominee holdings are also used to circumvent the restrictions on foreign shareholding, which the law prohibits and punishes as a criminal offence.

A frequent problem arises when the Beneficial Owner wishes to reclaim shares from the Nominee, only to find it difficult to compel the transfer, because the shareholder register names the Nominee and is presumed to be correct evidence (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1141), so the Beneficial Owner must prove the nominee agreement before the shares can be recovered.

This article analyses the relevant legal framework, the legal risks involved, and preventive measures for business owners, with reference to statutory provisions.

2. Legal Framework

2.1 The Shareholder Register as Key Evidence

The Civil and Commercial Code (CCC) establishes key principles regarding share transfers and the shareholder register under Section 1129:

"Shares are transferable without the consent of the company, unless in the case of shares with the holder's name inscribed on the share certificate, for which the company's regulations provide otherwise.

The transfer of shares with the holder's name inscribed on the share certificate is void unless made in writing and signed by both the transferor and the transferee, with at least one witness countersigning. The transfer instrument must also state the number of the shares transferred.

Such transfer cannot be set up against the company or third parties until the name and domicile of the transferee have been entered in the register of shareholders." Civil and Commercial Code, Section 1129
Key Legal Principle: A share transfer can only be enforced against the company and third parties once it has been recorded in the shareholder register. Therefore, the person named in the register may assert shareholder status against the company and third parties; between the transferor and the transferee, the effect depends on the juristic act the parties actually made.

2.2 Formal Requirements for Share Transfers

CCC Section 1129, paragraph 2, sets out clear formal requirements for transferring registered shares:

  1. Must be made in writing
  2. Signed by both the transferor and the transferee
  3. Countersigned by at least one witness
  4. Must state the share numbers being transferred

Failure to comply with these requirements renders the transfer void (CCC Section 1129, paragraph 2).

2.3 Director's Duty of Care

Where the Nominee also serves as a director of the company, the law imposes a duty of care:

"In conducting the business of the company, the directors must exercise the care of a prudent businessperson." Civil and Commercial Code, Section 1168, paragraph 1

If a director causes damage to the company, shareholders may bring a derivative action under Section 1169:

"If a director causes damage to the company, the company may sue the director for compensation. If the company refuses to bring the action, any shareholder may bring the case instead." Civil and Commercial Code, Section 1169, paragraph 1

2.4 Simulation and Concealed Juristic Acts

Nominee shareholding arrangements may constitute a concealed juristic act under CCC Section 155:

"A declaration of intention made in collusion with the other party is void, but this cannot be set up against a third party who acts in good faith and suffers damage from such simulated declaration.

If the simulated declaration under paragraph 1 is made to conceal another juristic act, the provisions of the law governing the concealed juristic act shall apply." Civil and Commercial Code, Section 155

3. Legal Analysis

3.1 Hypothetical Scenario

Scenario: Asking a Friend to Hold Shares

"We" founded a company operating a profitable beauty services business. Due to personal problems, we transferred all our shares to "Friend", a close friend and co-founder, with a verbal agreement that Friend would hold the shares temporarily on our behalf.

When we resolved our personal issues and asked Friend to transfer the shares back, Friend refused, claiming to be the legal shareholder as recorded in the shareholder register, and that the verbal agreement had no binding effect.

3.2 Legal Issues

# Issue Legal Basis
1 Are "We" still a shareholder of the company? CCC Section 1129 (Shareholder Register)
2 Is the verbal nominee agreement legally enforceable? CCC Section 155 (Simulation / Concealed Act)
3 Can "We" sue to reclaim the shares, and what must be proved? Civil and Commercial Code Section 155; burden of proof under Civil Procedure Code Section 84/1
4 If "Friend" mismanages the company, what remedies are available? Civil and Commercial Code Section 1169 (Derivative Action) and Civil and Commercial Code Section 1141

3.3 IRAC Analysis

Issue 1: Legal Status of the Beneficial Owner

Under Civil and Commercial Code Section 1129, paragraph 3, once a transfer has been entered in the shareholder register, the transferee may assert shareholder status against the company and third parties; between the transferor and the transferee, the effect depends on the juristic act the parties actually made. Once "We" transferred shares to "Friend" in compliance with the formal requirements and the transfer was recorded in the register, "Friend" may assert shareholder status against the company and third parties. If the transfer was genuine, "We" ceased to be a shareholder. If, however, the transfer was a simulated declaration of intention made so that "Friend" would hold the shares on our behalf, it is void between the parties (Civil and Commercial Code Section 155, paragraph 1), and "We" may rely on that against "Friend", but not against a third party who acted in good faith and would suffer damage from the simulation.

Issue 2: Effect of the Verbal Agreement

The verbal agreement that "Friend" would hold shares on behalf of "We" may constitute a concealed juristic act (CCC Section 155, paragraph 2). The apparent transaction is a "share sale," while the true transaction is a "nominee shareholding arrangement," which must be governed by the rules of the concealed transaction.

However, the critical problem is the burden of proof: "We", as the party alleging the nominee arrangement, must prove it (Civil Procedure Code Section 84/1). Even where the law requires documentary evidence, the final part of Civil Procedure Code Section 94 does not prevent a party from calling witnesses to show that a contract or other obligation stated in a document is not valid, so written evidence makes proof easier; without it, proof becomes difficult.

Critical Risk: Without written evidence, "We" must rely on witness testimony, circumstantial evidence, or electronic evidence (e.g., LINE messages, emails) to prove the nominee arrangement. Courts will weigh such evidence on a case-by-case basis.

Issue 3: Right to Reclaim Shares

If "We" can prove the share transfer was a concealed juristic act, the following legal avenues are available:

  • Sue for enforcement of the agency agreement (CCC Sections 797 and 810)
  • Sue for return of property held without right (CCC Section 1336)
  • Sue for a declaration that the share transfer made by a simulated declaration of intention is void (Civil and Commercial Code Section 155, paragraph 1) and for an order that the Nominee return the shares; the return of property arising from a void act is governed by the provisions on undue enrichment (Civil and Commercial Code Section 172)

Issue 4: Damage Caused by the Nominee's Management

If "Friend" is a director and causes damage to the company, the company may sue the director for compensation and, where the company refuses to sue, any shareholder may bring the action (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1169). The right belongs to shareholders; "We", not being in the register, must first rebut the presumption that the register is correct evidence (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1141). If "Friend" is not a director, Civil and Commercial Code Section 1169 does not apply to Friend's conduct and "We" must rely on the agreement with "Friend".

3.4 Relevant Statutory Rules

Provision Legal Principle
Civil and Commercial Code Section 1169, paragraph 1 Where the company refuses to sue a director who has caused it damage, any shareholder may bring the action; a person who is no longer a shareholder is not a "shareholder" within the meaning of the provision.
Civil and Commercial Code Section 1015 and Section 1169 The claim for damage caused by a director belongs to the company, a juristic person separate from its shareholders; a shareholder may exercise it only where the company refuses to sue.
Civil and Commercial Code Section 1237 The court may order a limited company to be dissolved on the grounds in Civil and Commercial Code Section 1237, for example where its business can only be carried on at a loss with no hope of recovery (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1237(3)) or where any other cause makes it impossible for the company to continue in existence (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1237(5)). Mismanagement by directors may only be a fact supporting such a ground.

4. Risk Summary of Nominee Shareholding

Level Risk Details
High Loss of ownership A Beneficial Owner who is not named in the shareholder register cannot exercise shareholder rights against the company directly, but may still require the Nominee to transfer the shares back under the agreement and the law of agency, if the agreement can be proved.
High Burden of proof on the Beneficial Owner Must prove the nominee arrangement existed, which is extremely difficult without written evidence.
High Nominee transfers shares to third parties Third parties who act in good faith and would suffer damage from the simulation are protected, because the Beneficial Owner cannot set up the voidness of the transfer against them (CCC Section 155, paragraph 1).
Medium Nominee votes to alter the company Changes to directors, capital increases, or bylaw amendments without the Beneficial Owner's consent.
Medium Share dilution The Nominee votes by special resolution for a capital increase (Section 1220) and then declines the new shares that must be offered to shareholders in proportion to their holdings, so the directors may sell them to other shareholders or take them up themselves (Section 1222), diluting the original shareholding.
Medium Lengthy litigation Lawsuits to reclaim shares may take years, during which the Beneficial Owner has no management authority.

5. Preventive Recommendations for Business Owners

5.1 Never Use Nominee Arrangements Without a Written Agreement

If nominee shareholding is unavoidable, the following steps should be taken:

  1. Execute a written Nominee Agreement specifying share details, return conditions, and the reason for the arrangement.
  2. Prepare a share transfer instrument signed in advance by the Nominee as transferor that meets the form required by Civil and Commercial Code Section 1129, paragraph 2: made in writing, signed by both transferor and transferee, with at least one witness certifying the signatures, and stating the numbers of the shares transferred. When the shares are to be returned, the transfer is entered in the company's register of shareholders. The risk that the Nominee will dispute an instrument signed in advance remains.
  3. Have the Nominee appoint the Beneficial Owner as proxy to vote on the Nominee's behalf, in writing (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1187), dated and signed by the shareholder, stating the number of shares, the proxy's name and the meeting or period for which the proxy is appointed (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1188). The Nominee, as principal, may revoke the appointment at any time (Civil and Commercial Code Section 827), so a proxy is not a secure safeguard.
  4. Preserve all communications including electronic messages, emails, and meeting minutes.

5.2 Safer Structural Alternatives

Alternative Advantages Limitations
Shareholders' Agreement Clearly defines rights, duties, and share transfer conditions. Binding only between the parties, not enforceable against third parties.
Share Transfer Restrictions in the Articles of Association The articles of association may restrict the transfer of shares with the holder's name inscribed on the share certificate (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1129, paragraph 1); the restriction binds all shareholders. The articles must be amended by special resolution (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1145) and the amendment registered within fourteen days (Civil and Commercial Code Section 1146). The restriction does not give the Beneficial Owner any right in the shares.
Call Option Grants the Beneficial Owner the right to repurchase shares at a pre-agreed price. A written agreement or option is advisable as evidence (Civil and Commercial Code Section 454), and on exercise the shares must be transferred in the form required by Civil and Commercial Code Section 1129, paragraph 2.
Share Pledge A share pledge secures the performance of a debt (Civil and Commercial Code Section 747); a pledge of share certificates with the holder's name inscribed cannot be set up against the company or third parties unless registered in the company's books (Civil and Commercial Code Section 753). Does not confer voting rights at meetings; the pledgor remains the shareholder, so a pledge is not an alternative to a nominee holding.

5.3 What Not to Do

Important Warnings:
  • Never rely solely on verbal agreements — the Beneficial Owner bears the burden of proving the nominee agreement (Civil Procedure Code Section 84/1), and without written evidence that proof is difficult.
  • Never trust personal relationships without protective mechanisms — personal bonds are not legal guarantees.
  • Never transfer 100% of shares to a single person — diversify risk or retain some shares in your own name.
  • Holding shares in a limited company on behalf of a foreigner, by a Thai national or a juristic person that is not a foreigner, so that the foreigner can operate a business in evasion or breach of the foreign business law is an offence under the Foreign Business Act B.E. 2542 (1999), Section 36, for the nominee and for the foreigner who consents, punishable by imprisonment of up to three years, or a fine of 100,000 to 1,000,000 Baht, or both. The court shall order the shareholding to be terminated, and failure to comply with that order is punishable by a fine of 10,000 to 50,000 Baht for each day of non-compliance. A nominee agreement made for this purpose is also at risk of being void under Section 150 of the Civil and Commercial Code.

6. Conclusion

Nominee shareholding without a written agreement is one of the most serious legal risks for Thai business owners. The law gives primacy to the shareholder register. A person not named in the register cannot exercise shareholder rights against the company directly, even as the true owner, but may still require the Nominee to transfer the shares back under the agreement and the law of agency, if the agreement can be proved.

In practice, if a shareholding structure involves other persons, proper written agreements, appropriate protective mechanisms, and professional legal advice should be secured to ensure all parties' rights are adequately protected.

Key Takeaway:
"If your name does not appear in the shareholder register, you cannot exercise shareholder rights against the company directly, whether you founded it, invested in it, or conceived the idea, and you must prove the nominee agreement to recover the shares."
